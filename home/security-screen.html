TL;DR: what I'd do

Important first: to leave the glass open overnight, the screen panel itself has to be the security barrier. That means woven stainless mesh clamped (not splined) into a heavy aluminum frame, a keyed or multi-point lock, and anti-lift hardware. A "heavy-duty" bug screen with pet mesh won't do it, even with a better latch.

Add 12×12 woven stainless (Metro Screenworks 48"×12' roll, $199.71 (source)), retained with a screwed aluminum clamp strip instead of spline, plus a keyed lock and anti-lift blocks. Ballpark parts: $450–$700. Weak points: thin .043" frame walls, nylon corners, home-made mesh retention, and no tested lock. Details in the DIY section.

Also do this regardless (≈$20–$50): add anti-lift protection to the glass slider. Use a Prime-Line anti-lift bolt ($7.63–$9.99) or keyed pin lock ($8.23), plus a charley bar ($19–$38) (source, source).

Moving in 1–2 years? A $2–2.5k stainless slider is a fixture that normally stays with the house. Treat it as a home upgrade you'll enjoy for two cool seasons that also helps the listing, not as something to take along (see the resale section). HOA: rear sliders are usually low-visibility, but many East Valley HOAs still want architectural approval for exterior color or frame changes. Ask for the form and pick a frame color matching your existing door frame.

Measuring checklist (do this before any quote or order)

Tools: a steel tape measure, 1" wide blade if you have one (Best Custom Screens recommends a ≥1" blade, e.g., Stanley FatMax 25'), a flashlight, a phone camera, and painter's tape for labeling. Record everything to the nearest 1/16".

A. Identify the setup

  • Stand outside. Which side does the glass door open? Note "opens left" or "opens right" as seen from outside.
  • Where does the current screen ride: outside the glass (most common) or inside? Is it on its own outermost track lip?
  • Number of glass panels (2-panel XO/OX, 3-panel, or 4-panel OXXO). Which panels move?
  • Door frame material and color (aluminum, vinyl, fiberglass). Note brand stickers or etching in the glass corner (e.g., Milgard, PGT, Anlin).

B. Measure the opening the screen uses

  • Height at 3 points (left, center, right): from the top of the bottom screen-track rail up to the underside (bottom lip) of the top U-channel. Best Custom Screens' method: take this reading and add 1/4" for their door size. Write down the raw numbers anyway and let the vendor apply their own rule.
  • Height inside the top channel: also measure from the bottom-track rail to the inside top of the head channel. That tells the vendor how much lift room exists (critical for anti-lift blocks).
  • Width at 3 heights (top, middle, bottom): jamb to jamb across the full opening, plus the width of the current screen panel itself.
  • Diagonals corner to corner both ways. A difference over ~1/4" means the opening is out of square, so tell the installer.
  • Note the smallest height and width; custom shops build to the tightest point.

C. Track details

  • Bottom track: is the screen rail a separate raised fin/rib, and how tall is it (e.g., 1/4", 3/8")? Is it an aluminum rail, or a stainless cap over aluminum?
  • Top channel: depth (how far up the screen frame sits inside it) and inside width.
  • Distance from the screen track to the glass panel's outer face, so the new frame thickness clears the glass door handle.
  • Is there a flat surface around the opening, at least ~2-1/8" wide per the Titan surface-mount guide? This only matters if a vendor proposes a surface-mount kit.
  • Condition: dents, bent fin, cracked or missing sections, years of dust packed in?

D. Current screen hardware

  • Lift the current screen out and look at the rollers: top tension rollers or none? Bottom roller diameter (e.g., 1-1/4")? Steel ball-bearing or plastic? Adjustment-screw location?
  • Frame profile depth and thickness of the current screen panel (e.g., 1/2" × 1-3/4").
  • Latch type and where the keeper/strike is on the jamb.

E. Photos to take (send these with every quote request)

  • Whole door from outside, square-on, with the screen closed; then the same with the screen open.
  • Close-ups of the bottom track end-on (tape-measure in frame for scale) and the top channel from below.
  • Both jambs (latch side and interlock side), plus the current latch and keeper.
  • The rollers on the old screen panel, and any brand label or glass etching.
  • Wider shot showing the patio, stucco or trim, and frame color (for HOA match and installer planning).

Locks & anti-lift

Screen-door lock types

  • Hook / J-hook latch (basic): what's on most bug screens and the heavy-duty sliders. A pry bar pops it. Not security.
  • Keyed mortise latch (single point): e.g., the flush mortise keyed latch on Best Custom Screens' security slider. It's a real lock but has one point of failure. Better when paired with anti-lift blocks.
  • Hook-bolt lock: a hooked bolt swings into the jamb, so the panel can't be slid or levered away. It's the standard slider lock in Australian security doors. Recommended for sliders because the slider's weak point is being lifted or levered off the track (source).
  • Multi-point (3-point) lock: engages top, middle, and bottom. Used by UHD/Titan, Sure Secured, Paramount, Boss, Bulldog, and Crimsafe. This is the one to ask for.
  • Single vs. double cylinder: single cylinder means key outside, thumb-turn inside. Double cylinder means key on both sides, so a hand through a hole can't reach a thumb-turn, but you need the key to get out in a fire. UHD's 2-panel kit puts single-cylinder on the active panel and double-cylinder on the fixed one (source). For a patio exit, I'd pick single-cylinder, or keep a key on a hook out of reach of the mesh.
  • Ask for a re-keyable cylinder (UHD uses a Kwikset keyway) so it can match your house key.

Anti-lift for the screen panel

  • Anti-lift blocks or pins in the head channel take up the gap above the panel, so it can't be lifted high enough to clear the bottom rail. Crimsafe lists anti-lift pins, and Paramount stresses they must be set into the track correctly (source, source).
  • Interlock along the fixed-panel side hooks the moving panel to the frame (UHD/Titan "panel interlock").
  • Even a 1/8" gap at the top can defeat a multi-point lock (Paramount). That's why precise measuring matters.

Anti-lift for the glass door itself (cheap, do it anyway)

  • Charley bar: Ideal Security patio bar with anti-lift lock, $19.10–$38.49 depending on color, adjustable 25.75–47.5", and it can hold the door partially open for ventilation (source). Its "anti-lift" refers to the bar, not the door.
  • Anti-lift bolt: Prime-Line U-9866 $7.63 / U-9867 $9.99 (source).
  • Keyed pin lock: Prime-Line S-4005, $8.23, hardened steel bolt that resists prying and lifting (source). LOCKiT! double-bolt lock, $29.95 (source).
  • Anti-lift screws in the upper track of the glass door cost almost nothing and stop the panel being lifted off (source). All of these only work with the glass closed (or bar-limited), which is why the screen has to carry the security when the glass is open.

  • 304 vs 316: 316 adds molybdenum for salt resistance. Inland Mesa doesn't need it, and 304 is fine. What matters more is wire diameter (security grade is ~0.8–0.9 mm, about .031–.035") and how the mesh is clamped in the frame (source). Ask every vendor to put alloy + wire diameter in writing.
  • Hardware-store "stainless security screen" rolls are often thinner (Metro's is .023" wire, 12×12). It's tough against dogs and golf balls, but thinner than the 0.8–0.9 mm security systems.
  • Dog doors: some shops add one (Boss mentions doggie doors, Paramount says it's possible). Others refuse because cutting the mesh voids the warranty (source). A dog flap is also a security hole, so skip it if the goal is "glass open overnight."
  • Pushing: a dog that body-slams the screen can pop a light bug-screen panel off its track. A security slider's anti-lift blocks and interlock fix that too.

Arizona durability

  • Frame: powder-coated extruded aluminum (6063-T5 architectural grade is typical) won't rust. Avoid painted steel frames, which need repainting in AZ sun (Boss says every ~24 months for iron) (source). Light colors (desert sand, almond, white) run cooler to the touch than black or bronze on a west or south exposure. Black mesh gives the clearest view.
  • Heat warping: thin roll-formed bug-screen frames bow in summer and jump the track. Heavier extrusions (security frames, or at least 2" or larger heavy-duty frames) resist it. Ask about the warranty on powder coat and fading.
  • UV: vinyl and fiberglass meshes degrade, while stainless doesn't. Rollers and weatherstrip are the wear parts; ask for stainless-steel ball-bearing rollers.
  • Dust and monsoon: grit in the bottom track wears rollers and makes heavy security panels drag. Vacuum the track monthly in dust season. Wipe the rail, and use a dry silicone spray (Best Custom Screens suggests silicone or Aluma-Lub), not oil or grease that holds dirt. Rinse the mesh with a hose and mild soap a few times a year (helps 304 avoid tea-staining). Check roller height adjustment yearly.
  • Solar bonus: stainless mesh vendors claim roughly 52–66% heat or UV reduction (DXN/Crimsafe 52%, Titan 60% UV, Boss 66%). Treat these as vendor claims, but expect some reduction on a sunny slider.

DIY path (honest version)

Short answer: you can DIY a very tough, dog-proof, keyed screen. You can't easily DIY a tested security screen. The pros' strength comes from the system: thick-walled security extrusions, a screw-clamp or wedge mesh retention that's been tested, reinforced corners, multi-point locks throwing into solid structure, and anti-lift. "A stronger wire in a weaker frame still fails at the frame" (source). No US retail DIY kit with security-grade clamp extrusions turned up in my research; Crimsafe and Meshtec frames are dealer-only.

Build notes

  1. Spline vs. clamp: security mesh "is not designed to be rolled into a standard aluminum screen frame" (Metro). Spline-held stainless pulls out under a push or kick. Instead, lay the mesh over the frame face and sandwich it under a screwed aluminum strip all the way round. That's a home-made version of the screw-clamp idea.
  2. Add a mid-rail or cross-brace. A wide mesh panel acts like a sail and racks the frame.
  3. Through-bolt or rivet the corners. Nylon corner keys are the weakest point.
  4. Set the lock keeper into the door frame structure, not just the trim, and fit anti-lift blocks so the panel can't rise more than ~1/8".

Where DIY is weaker (be honest with yourself)

  • A thin-wall .043" frame bends under a pry bar long before stainless mesh fails.
  • Thinner hardware-store wire (.023") versus 0.8–0.9 mm security wire.
  • Untested retention and lock, no break-in warranty, and no resale credibility as a "security screen."
  • A heavier panel on stock-style rollers wears faster in AZ dust.

Verdict: for Frank's goal (glass open overnight, unattended), the DIY build is a solid dog-proof, deterrent screen for ~25% of the pro price, but I wouldn't rely on it as a true security barrier. If DIY appeals mainly for cost, the $2,149 ship-to-home Sure Secured panel installed yourself is the better "DIY install."

1–2 year move: cost, resale, take-it-or-leave-it

  • It'll likely stay. A custom security slider is built to your exact track and attached to the house. It normally conveys as a fixture, and it won't fit your next home's slider. If you'd want to take it, it must be explicitly excluded in your listing and purchase contract; confirm with your agent. Practically, plan on leaving it.
  • Resale appeal: in the Phoenix area, stainless security screens are a recognized upgrade (security, pets, AC savings). Expect it to help listing appeal and showings more than to return its full cost in sale price. No reliable local resale-ROI data was found, and vendor claims of "increased property value" are marketing.
  • Cost per season: about $2,100–$2,700 for 2–4 cool seasons (Oct–Apr) of "glass open overnight." If that's worth it to you, buy the real thing. If not, the budget combo (pet-screen heavy-duty slider + charley bar vent gap + anti-lift pins, under ~$500) gets most of the airflow benefit while home, at a fraction of the cost, and the cheap parts go with you or stay without regret.
  • Ask about warranty transfer. Boss advertises a transferable 15-year parts and labor warranty (per a competitor's summary of Boss's terms (source)). A transferable warranty is a nice line item in a listing.
  • HOA: before ordering, get your HOA's architectural request form if exterior changes require one. Pick a frame color that matches the existing slider frame. Most installers supply spec sheets and color chips for submissions.

What still needs Frank's input

  • Measurements and photos per the checklist (width, height ×3, track profile, rail height, head-channel depth, rollers, which side opens).
  • Your door width: it decides whether the Best Custom Screens security model is available in your size, and confirms the UHD/Titan 23–52" track-mount range.
  • Whether the existing screen rides outside the glass in its own track, and whether the track is in good shape (a bent fin may push toward a new-track install).
  • Frame color and HOA rules (CC&Rs or architectural form).
  • Dog size and behavior: a jumper or pusher argues more strongly for a stainless, anti-lift system.
  • Lock preference: single-cylinder (thumb-turn inside) vs double-cylinder.
